Case Study · Final Year Project

Smart Home Security

An IoT intrusion detection system built around a simple idea: an alert is only useful if it arrives in time and brings evidence with it.

— a notification without proof is just anxiety
Role End-to-end design & development
Context Final Year Project
Timeline Sept 2024 – Apr 2025
Focus IoT, cloud storage & AI analysis

The problem

Consumer home security tends to fail in one of two directions. Motion sensors alone fire constantly — a pet, a curtain, a passing car — and people learn to ignore them. Camera systems record everything faithfully but leave you scrubbing through hours of footage after the fact to work out what actually happened.

Both failures share a root cause: the system reports events but does not interpret them. It tells you something moved, not whether that something mattered. And when several people share a home, everyone typically gets the same undifferentiated stream of alerts regardless of whether they can act on them.

The real brief

Not "detect motion" — decide whether an event is worth someone's attention, and hand them the evidence to judge it themselves.

Approach

I treated it as a pipeline rather than a set of features: a trigger has to become an image, an image has to become an assessment, and an assessment has to reach the right person quickly enough to matter. Each stage was built and tested on its own before being chained to the next.

  • Sensor layer — IoT sensors detect entry events and act purely as triggers, keeping the detection logic itself off the constrained device.
  • Cloud image capture — a trigger causes a capture that is uploaded immediately, so evidence survives even if the local hardware is tampered with.
  • AI forensic analysis — captured images are analysed to distinguish a genuine intrusion from routine activity, which is what keeps alerts meaningful.
  • Role-based access control — household members receive different permissions, so administration and casual viewing are properly separated.

Decisions worth explaining

Cloud storage rather than local recording

Captured images upload to cloud storage the moment they are taken, instead of being written to a device in the house.

Trade-off: the system depends on connectivity and incurs storage cost. In exchange, evidence cannot be destroyed by taking the hardware — which is precisely what an intruder would do.

Capture on trigger, not continuous recording

The camera captures in response to a sensor event rather than recording a permanent stream.

Trade-off: anything between triggers goes unrecorded. It keeps bandwidth and storage viable, and leaves a reviewable set of moments instead of hours nobody will watch.

AI analysis before notification

Images are assessed before an alert is raised, so the notification carries a judgement rather than only a timestamp.

Trade-off: adds latency between the event and the alert, and the model can be wrong. A system that cries wolf gets muted, which is a worse failure than a short delay.

Roles instead of a single shared login

Access is scoped by role, separating who can administer the system from who can simply view alerts.

Trade-off: more setup than one shared password. Necessary once a system holds images of people's homes — shared credentials make access impossible to reason about.

What I built

  • An IoT detection layer that registers unauthorized access events and triggers the capture pipeline.
  • Cloud image storage, so every triggered event leaves a durable, tamper-resistant record.
  • An AI forensic analysis step that assesses captured images to separate genuine intrusions from ordinary activity.
  • Real-time alerting that reaches the right household member with the supporting image attached.
  • Role-based access control governing who can administer the system and who can view its history.

Outcome

The result is a working prototype that takes an intrusion event all the way through to a real-time alert backed by a stored image trail. It was developed and submitted over the course of a year as my final year project, and it is the piece of work that most shaped how I think about system design — specifically, that the hard part is rarely detection, it is deciding what deserves someone's attention.
