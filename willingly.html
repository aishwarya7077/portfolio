Case Study · Product & Frontend

Willingly

A donation platform for people who want to give but can't tell which nonprofits deserve it. Built trust-first: every organisation is verified before it can accept a rupee.

— trust first, features second
Role Product & Frontend Engineering
Scope MVP — Phase 1
Architecture API-first modular monolith
Status In development

The problem

Someone decides to donate. They search, find a dozen organisations with earnest homepages, and then hit the actual question: which of these is real, and where does the money go? Nothing on the page answers it. Every nonprofit claims impact. Registration numbers, if shown at all, are text on a page that nobody has checked.

The result is that the intent to give doesn't convert. People either fall back to the two or three household names they already recognise, or they give up. Smaller organisations doing good work stay invisible because they have no way to prove they are trustworthy.

The framing that shaped everything

Trust isn't a feature you add to a donation platform — it's the product. So the build order put verification and transparency first and left the interesting extras for later.

Approach

Phase 1 was scoped as a deliberately small MVP: enough product for a real donor to find a real nonprofit and complete a real donation, and nothing beyond that. The scope was written down as much for what it excluded as what it included.

  • Accounts and auth — registration and login for donors and nonprofit staff, with roles kept distinct from day one.
  • Nonprofit onboarding — organisations submit their profile and supporting documents, then wait for review. Nothing goes live unreviewed.
  • Admin portal — an internal queue for approving or rejecting registrations, which is the gate the whole trust model hangs on.
  • Browse and search — discovery across verified organisations and their campaigns.
  • Campaigns — nonprofits raise for something specific rather than into a general fund.
  • Donation flow — Stripe end to end, with the payment state handled properly rather than optimistically.
  • Donor dashboard — a complete history of what you gave and to whom.

Explicitly deferred: AI recommendations, advanced analytics, mobile apps, social features, multi-country compliance, and complex fundraising workflows. Each is defensible eventually; none of them helps a first user complete a first donation.

Decisions worth explaining

A modular monolith, not microservices

The domain boundaries — accounts, organisations, campaigns, donations — are modelled as separate modules inside one deployable application, with the seams drawn where services would eventually split.

Trade-off: gives up independent scaling per service, which an MVP with no traffic does not need. Buys a single thing to run, migrate, and debug — and the boundaries stay available if the split is ever worth doing.

API-first, so mobile isn't a rewrite

Every capability is exposed through the FastAPI layer, and the Next.js frontend is just its first consumer. No business logic lives only in the web client.

Trade-off: more upfront design than rendering straight from the server. Means the deferred mobile app is a new client against a working API rather than a backend rewrite.

Verification as a state machine, not a flag

A nonprofit moves through explicit states — submitted, under review, approved, rejected — and only an approved organisation is visible in search or able to receive donations.

Trade-off: a human review queue is a real operational cost and a growth bottleneck. That bottleneck is the point: an unreviewed listing would make the platform's core promise meaningless.

Stripe holds the money, we hold the record

Card data never touches the application. Stripe owns the payment; the database records the donation and reconciles against Stripe's webhooks as the source of truth.

Trade-off: processing fees and dependence on an external provider. Removes PCI scope entirely, which for a small team handling other people's charitable money is not a close call.

Managed auth instead of rolling our own

Authentication is delegated to Clerk, with the application keeping only the role and permission model that is actually specific to this domain.

Trade-off: a vendor in the critical login path. Avoids hand-writing session handling, password resets, and account recovery — the classic places small teams ship security bugs.

What I built

My work spanned the product surface and the frontend implementation, with enough backend involvement to keep the API contract honest.

  • The nonprofit onboarding flow — a multi-step form for profile details and document upload, with save-and-resume so an organisation isn't forced to finish in one sitting.
  • The admin review queue, where each submission is presented with its documents side by side and approve/reject is one deliberate action with a reason attached.
  • Browse and search across verified organisations, with filtering by cause and location, and empty states that suggest a next step rather than showing a blank page.
  • Campaign pages carrying the goal, progress, and the organisation's verification status in the same view as the donate button.
  • The donation flow against Stripe — including the unglamorous parts: pending states, failed payments, and a receipt that arrives whether or not the browser stayed open.
  • The donor dashboard, a plain, complete history of past donations.
Detail I'd defend

The verification badge links to what was actually checked, rather than being a decorative tick. A trust signal that can't be interrogated is just decoration — and the whole platform exists to avoid that.

How it was built

  • PostgreSQL with SQLAlchemy models and Alembic migrations, so schema changes are reviewable and reversible.
  • Docker for local development — the whole stack comes up with one command, which keeps onboarding a new contributor to minutes.
  • Unit tests on the core business logic: donation state transitions, verification state changes, and permission checks.
  • Feature branches and pull requests, with every feature deployable and independently testable rather than merged into a long-running integration branch.
  • S3-compatible object storage for verification documents, kept out of the database and off the public path.

Where it stands

Phase 1 is a working product rather than a prototype: a donor can register, find a verified nonprofit, give through Stripe, and see the donation in their history — and no organisation reaches that list without passing human review first.

The next phase earns its features from real usage rather than a roadmap written in advance — which is why recommendations and analytics were left out of Phase 1 rather than half-built into it.
